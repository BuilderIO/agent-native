A consulting deck gives a client the answer to their question first, then the argument and proof behind it, with one takeaway per slide. You build it in words before slides: the question, the answer, then every slide title as a full sentence. This guide walks through one made-up engagement from the client's question to the finished slides.

## What you need

- The client's question
- Notes from client interviews
- Your analysis and its data
- Who decides, and whose teams the answer affects

## How to make a consulting deck

### 1. Write the client's question and the decision

A consulting deck answers one question, so **write the client's question as one sentence**. Under it, write the decision your answer feeds and who makes it. If you can't fit the question in one sentence, the engagement has more than one question, and each needs its own section or deck.

In the example used throughout this guide, Tidewater Fitness, a made-up chain of 40 gyms, is losing members. Its question is "Should we cut membership prices to stop members leaving?" The decision is whether the CEO puts a price cut in next year's budget.

### 2. Write the answer first

The deck opens with your answer, because executives read the start and may stop there. Read your interview notes and analysis for what they add up to, then **write the answer as one sentence the decision-maker could act on**. Under it, write the three reasons that support it. This is the answer-first structure Barbara Minto set out in her book *The Pyramid Principle*.

Tidewater's answer is "Keep prices as they are and fix members' first 90 days instead." Three reasons support it. Most members leave in those 90 days, and price isn't the main reason they leave. Members who build a class habit in their first month stay.

The reasons shouldn't overlap, and together they should cover the answer. Consultants call this MECE: mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive.

If your analysis isn't finished, write the answer you expect. The ghost deck in [step 4](#4-sketch-a-ghost-deck) shows which proof you still need.

### 3. Write the storyline as slide titles

Give every slide an **action title**, which tells the reader what to conclude from that slide. "Cancellations" only names what the slide covers. "61% of cancellations happen in the first 90 days" says what the data shows.

**Write every title before you build any slide**, under the reason it supports:

| Section | Slide title |
|---|---|
| Answer | Keep prices as they are and fix members' first 90 days |
| Reason 1 | 61% of cancellations happen in the first 90 days |
| Reason 2 | Members who joined on a discount cancel at the same rate as everyone else |
| Reason 2 | Cancelling members rank price fourth, behind not knowing where to start |
| Reason 3 | Members who book three classes in month one are nearly twice as likely to stay a year |
| Next steps | Book every new member into three classes, starting with an 8-gym pilot |

The titles alone, read top to bottom, make the whole argument.

### 4. Sketch a ghost deck

A **ghost deck** is the deck with titles and no content yet. Make one blank slide per title, then **write on each slide the chart that would prove it**, where its data comes from, and whether you have it yet. Tidewater's ghost deck halfway through the analysis:

| Slide title | Proof it needs | Data | Have it? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 61% of cancellations happen in the first 90 days | Cancellations by time as a member | Membership system | Yes |
| Cancelling members rank price fourth, behind not knowing where to start | Main reason for cancelling, ranked | Exit survey | Not yet |
| Members who book three classes in month one are nearly twice as likely to stay a year | Retention by classes booked in month one | Bookings and membership | Not yet |

The "not yet" rows are your remaining analysis. If the data disproves a title, rewrite the title and check that the answer still holds.

### 5. Prove each title with one chart

Each slide body proves its title with one chart or table. **Highlight the number the title states** in the accent color and keep the rest neutral. Pick the chart for the comparison the title makes:

| The title compares | Use |
|---|---|
| A change over time | Line chart |
| Items ranked by size | Horizontal bar chart |
| Parts of a whole | Stacked bar |
| Two groups or before and after | Two bars side by side |
| Exact figures people will look up | Table |

Add **a source line** at the bottom of every slide. It names the data, its date range and any filter, such as "Tidewater membership system, 2024–2025 cancellations, 40 gyms." Keep the title at the top and the source at the bottom of every slide. If a slide needs two charts to make its point, split it into two slides with two titles.

### 6. Write the executive summary last

Write the executive summary after the body slides, and **put it right after the title slide**. It is the storyline in short form: the answer, then each reason in one sentence with its strongest number. Someone who reads only this slide should know what you recommend and why.

Tidewater's summary opens with "A price cut wouldn't stop cancellations", then three lines:

- 61% of cancellations happen in the first 90 days.
- Discount joiners cancel at the same rate, and members rank price fourth.
- Members who book three classes in month one stay a year 74% of the time, against 41% of those who book fewer.

### 7. Close with recommendations and next steps

Turn the answer into actions, **each with an owner and a date**. Tidewater's recommendation is to book every new member into three classes in their first month, starting with a pilot in 8 gyms. The head of member experience starts it on April 1. The consultant compares 90-day cancellations with the other 32 gyms and reports on July 15, and the CEO decides on a rollout by August 1.

Move everything that supports the argument but doesn't drive it to an appendix after this slide: methods, full data tables, interview lists.

### 8. Read the titles only, then check every number

Test the deck in two passes. First, **read only the slide titles in order**: if the argument breaks, a title is missing or in the wrong place. Second, check every number on every slide against its source, using the date range and definition its source line gives.

Then ask "so what?" of each slide. If a slide doesn't change what the client should believe or do, move it to the appendix.

### 9. Pre-wire the deck before the meeting

**Walk the decision-maker through the deck before the meeting**, along with anyone whose team the answer affects. Consultants call this pre-wiring. It finds objections while you can still answer them, so the meeting is about the decision rather than people's first reaction to the answer.

Send the deck ahead as a pre-read. Clients forward a consulting deck to people who weren't in the room, and full-sentence titles with source lines let it make the argument without you.

## Common problems

### The titles don't add up to the answer

The storyline has a gap or an extra point. List the three reasons under the answer and check each title belongs to exactly one reason. Add a title where a reason has no proof, and move a title that supports none to the appendix.

### The data disproves a slide title

The analysis came back different from the answer you expected. Rewrite the title to say what the data shows, then reread the storyline. If one reason no longer holds, change the answer before you build more slides.

### The client pushes back on the answer in the meeting

The decision-maker or their team saw the answer for the first time in the room. Pre-wire the next version with each objector. In the meeting, go to the slide that proves the point they question and walk through its source line.

### The deck is too long for the meeting

The deck carries detail the argument doesn't need. Keep the title, summary, one slide per reason and the recommendation in the main deck, and move the rest to the appendix. Present from the summary and open other slides only when asked.

## Questions

### How do you structure a consulting deck?

A consulting deck runs in five parts, in this order:

1. A title slide that states the answer
2. An executive summary of the answer and its reasons
3. One section per reason, each slide proving one action title
4. The recommendation, with owners and dates
5. An appendix with methods and full data

### What is a consulting deck?

A consulting deck is the presentation a consultant gives a client with the answer to the client's question, the findings behind it, and a recommendation. It leads with the answer, gives each slide a full-sentence title, and backs every claim with sourced data. It's written to be read as well as presented.

### What is a ghost deck in consulting?

A ghost deck is a draft of a consulting deck with slide titles but no content. Each slide holds its action title and a note of the chart or data that would prove it. Consultants use it to agree on the storyline early and to see which analysis is still missing.

### How many slides should a consulting deck have?

Enough slides for the argument, with one takeaway per slide. A main deck of 10 to 20 slides covers most client questions: title, executive summary, the findings and the recommendation. Methods, full data tables and interview lists go in an appendix after the recommendation, so the main deck stays short.

### What's the difference between a consulting deck and a capabilities deck?

A consulting deck answers one client's question with findings and a recommendation. A capabilities deck is a firm's overview of its own services, team and past work, used to win new clients. Agencies and consulting firms use both, at different stages: the capabilities deck before the work, the consulting deck during and after it.

When you pitch one prospect, [build the sales presentation from what they told you in discovery](https://www.agent-native.com/articles/sales-presentation/). For a recurring review with a client you already work with, see [how to build a QBR deck from the quarter's numbers](https://www.agent-native.com/articles/qbr-presentation/).

### What is the Pyramid Principle?

The Pyramid Principle is a way of structuring an argument with the conclusion first, then the reasons that support it, then the evidence for each reason. Barbara Minto developed it at McKinsey and published it as a book. Consulting decks follow it: the answer on the first slides, the findings after them, and the proof on each slide.
