To take good meeting notes, set up the page before the meeting and write down only decisions, action items and open questions. Give each action item an owner and a date, then send the cleaned-up notes soon after.

This guide follows one weekly product sync from the invite to the notes in everyone's inbox. Notes are the team's working record, less formal than meeting minutes and longer than the recap message you send afterwards.

## What you need

- The meeting invite or agenda
- A notes app or a notebook
- Where your team tracks tasks

## How to take meeting notes

### 1. Find out what the meeting has to decide

Notes are easier to take when you know what you're listening for. Read the invite and the agenda, and **write the meeting's purpose as one line**, such as "Agree on the onboarding checklist launch date". If the invite only has a title, ask the organizer what the meeting should decide.

In the example, Priya Raman takes notes at Marlowe Studio's weekly product sync. Its purpose line is "Confirm the onboarding launch date and this sprint's priorities."

### 2. Set up the page before the meeting

A page set up in advance means you only fill in blanks while people talk. **Put the agenda items in as headings**, under a header and three sections for what people will act on. Copy this layout into any notes app:

```text
Meeting name, date
Attendees:
Purpose:

Decisions
-

Action items (owner: task, date)
-

Open questions
-

1. First agenda item
-

2. Second agenda item
-
```

Priya's page has three agenda headings: onboarding checklist, export usage and pricing page copy. Use the same layout for every meeting you take notes in, so readers know where to look.

![The empty notes page for the weekly product sync, with the header filled in, empty Decisions, Action items and Open questions sections, and the three agenda items as headings.](https://cdn.builder.io/api/v1/image/assets%2FYJIGb4i01jvw0SRdL5Bt%2F03e045f0327d4ad1979e8b577711556f)

### 3. Listen for decisions, actions and open questions

Most of what's said in a meeting doesn't need writing down. **Write down only what someone will act on** or need to look up later. Give each kind a mark, so you can pull it out at a glance after the meeting:

| Listen for | It sounds like | Mark |
|---|---|---|
| A decision | "Let's go with…", "Agreed", "November 10 it is" | D: |
| An action item | "I'll take that", "Can you…", "by Friday" | A: |
| An open question | "We don't know yet", "Let's check with…" | ?: |
| A key fact or number | A date, a metric, a name | none |

Leave out the discussion that led to a decision. If the reason will matter later, add it in a few words after the decision. When the group seems to agree, **read the decision back in one sentence**, such as "So the checklist ships November 10?", and anyone who disagrees will say so.

### 4. Write fragments under each agenda item

Full sentences make you fall behind. **Write short fragments with names, numbers and dates** under the agenda item being discussed, such as "D: checklist ships Nov 10, after one more QA pass". When you miss something, write a dash and keep listening, then fill the gap after the meeting.

Under export usage, Priya's rough notes read: "usage up since last release, mostly CSV", "D: CSV redesign → next sprint" and "A: Daniel, post numbers in #product, Oct 28".

### 5. Give every action an owner and date

An action item with no owner doesn't get done. When someone says "we should…", **ask "Who's taking that, and by when?"** before the group moves on. Write each action in the same order every time: the owner, the task, the date.

At the sync, Priya asked who would run the final QA pass. Daniel Okafor took it, due November 6.

### 6. Clean up the notes right after the meeting

Right after the meeting, you still remember what each fragment meant. By the next day, you won't. **Clean up the notes within an hour of the meeting:**

1. Fill the gaps you marked with a dash.
2. Copy every D:, A: and ?: line into the sections at the top.
3. Check names, dates and numbers.
4. Cut fragments nobody will need.

If you're unsure what was decided, check the recording or ask the person who said it before you send anything. The top of Priya's finished notes, also shown in full at the start of this guide, reads:

```text
Decisions
- Onboarding checklist ships Nov 10, after one more QA pass
- CSV export redesign moves into next sprint

Action items
- Daniel: final QA pass on the onboarding checklist, Nov 6
- Daniel: post export usage numbers in #product, Oct 28
- Maya: send pricing page copy to legal, Oct 30

Open questions
- Which beta customers get the checklist first?
```

### 7. Send the notes and track the actions

**Send the notes to every attendee and anyone affected the same day.** Paste the decisions and action items into the message itself, where people see them without opening a file. Then add each action item to the tool your team tracks work in, with its owner and date, and save the notes where the team can search them later.

At the next meeting, start by going through last time's action items. Any item that slipped shows up before new work piles on top of it.

## Common problems

### You can't keep up with the conversation

You're writing too much. Switch to fragments, and write only the four kinds of note in [the listening table](#3-listen-for-decisions-actions-and-open-questions). When you fall behind, mark the gap with a dash and keep listening. If the group agrees, record the meeting so you can fill gaps from it afterwards.

### You're running the meeting and taking notes

While you steer the discussion, you can't also catch every owner and date. Ask someone else to take notes, and send them the page you set up before the meeting. If nobody can, record the meeting and write down only decisions and owners yourself.

### Nobody reads the notes

Usually, the decisions are buried under pages of discussion. Move decisions and action items to the top and cut what nobody will act on. Notes that arrive days later get skipped too, so send them the same day.

### People disagree later about what was decided

The decision was never stated out loud. From now on, read each decision back in the room before moving on. For this one, check the recording or the transcript if you have one, and send a correction to everyone who got the notes.

## Questions

### What should meeting notes include?

Meeting notes should include the date, the attendees, the meeting's purpose, the decisions made, the action items with an owner and a date for each, and any open questions. Short notes under each agenda item add the context. Leave out the back-and-forth that led to each decision.

### What's the difference between meeting notes and meeting minutes?

Meeting notes are an informal working record for the team: decisions, action items and open questions. Minutes are the formal record of a meeting, which boards and committees keep, with attendance, motions and votes. Minutes are approved at the next meeting.

### Can ChatGPT take meeting notes?

ChatGPT can turn a transcript or your rough notes into clean meeting notes, but it can't hear the meeting unless something records it first. An app that captures the call, such as Agent-Native Clips, gives you the transcript and a first draft of the notes. Check either one against what was said before you send it.

### Should I take meeting notes by hand or on a laptop?

Take notes by hand if it helps you listen and the meeting is in person, and on a laptop if you'll share or search them. Either way, type up the cleaned version after the meeting, because handwritten notes are hard to send and impossible to search.

### Which note-taking method works best for meetings?

For most meetings, agenda headings with a mark for each decision, action item and open question work best, because the notes follow the meeting's own order. The Cornell method splits a page into notes, cues and a summary, and suits lectures more than discussions. The outline method nests points under headings, which is close to agenda headings. Mind maps help in brainstorms but are hard to send.

### What are the 5 R's of note-taking?

The 5 R's come from the Cornell note-taking method: record, reduce, recite, reflect and review. For meeting notes, the first two matter most. Record the decisions and actions during the meeting, then reduce your notes to what people need when you clean them up.
