A meeting summary is a short note sent after a meeting that says what was decided, who's doing what by when, and what's still open. To write a meeting summary, **pull those three things out of your notes first** and add a two-sentence overview on top. Then check it against the record and send it the same day. To show how to summarize a meeting in practice, this guide follows one example from rough notes to the summary that goes out.

## What you need

- Your [meeting notes](https://www.agent-native.com/articles/meeting-notes/), recording or transcript
- The list of attendees
- The agenda, if there was one

A summary isn't the same as minutes or notes:

| | Meeting notes | Meeting summary | Meeting minutes |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | What you jot down during the meeting | The outcomes, sent after it | The official record |
| Who reads it | You | Attendees and people who missed it | The group, and anyone who checks later |
| How formal | Not at all | Short and plain | Formal, approved at the next meeting |

If you need the official record for a board or committee, follow [how to write meeting minutes](https://www.agent-native.com/articles/meeting-minutes/) instead.

## How to write a meeting summary

### 1. Decide who will read it

How much context a summary needs depends on who reads it. People who were in the meeting need the outcomes and nothing else. People who missed it need one line of background for each decision, so it makes sense without the discussion. A manager or a client, for example after a [quarterly business review](https://www.agent-native.com/articles/qbr-presentation/), needs the decisions and anything they have to approve.

When the summary goes to a mix of readers, **write it for the person who wasn't there**. The example in this guide is a website relaunch sync at a made-up coffee company, Fernhill Coffee. Its summary goes to the four attendees and to Lena, the head of marketing, who missed it.

### 2. Mark the decisions, actions and questions

Read through your notes once and **mark each line D, A or Q**: a decision, an action item or an open question. Unmarked lines are context, and most of them get cut. Here's the start of the Fernhill notes, marked up:

| Note | Mark |
|---|---|
| Launch moves from May 6 to May 20, payments vendor needs two more weeks to test the new checkout | D |
| Marcus: confirm the new date with the payments vendor | A |
| Long talk about homepage photos, Dana showed three options | – |
| Going with option B, the brighter one | D |
| Who writes the launch email? | Q |
| Dana to send final photo files to Marcus | A |
| Do we keep the old blog URLs? | Q |

Seven lines become two decisions, two action items and two open questions, and the photo discussion drops out.

### 3. Give each action an owner and date

An action item gets done when one named person owns it and knows when it's due. **Write each one as a task, a name and a date**: "Confirm the May 20 date with the payments vendor (Marcus, Friday)". Never use "we" or "the team" as the owner.

If the notes don't say who took a task, ask before you send. If no date came up, write "by next sync" rather than leaving it blank. Give each open question an owner too: the attendee who will get the answer. The Fernhill list now reads:

- Confirm the May 20 date with the payments vendor (Marcus, Friday)
- Send the final homepage photo files to Marcus (Dana, Wednesday)
- Who writes the launch email? (Theo asks Lena, who missed the sync, by Friday)
- Do old blog links redirect to the new site? (Marcus checks by next sync)

### 4. Write a two-sentence overview

The overview sits at the top, and some readers stop there. **Start with what was decided or what changed.** Leave out what was discussed and openers like "In today's meeting we covered…".

For Fernhill: "The relaunch moves to May 20 so the payments vendor can finish testing the new checkout. Homepage photos are picked, and two questions are still open: who writes the launch email and what happens to old blog links."

### 5. Add only the context a reader needs

Keep a piece of context only when someone would ask about it or act on it:

- The reason behind a decision, when someone will ask "why?" later
- A number someone will check, such as a budget or a date
- A link to the document, design or ticket the meeting was about
- The date of the next meeting, for a recurring one

Cut who said what, side topics and anything already settled before the meeting. In the Fernhill summary, the three photo options shrink to one line with a link to option B. **Put the parts in this order**: overview, decisions, action items, open questions, then links and the next meeting.

### 6. Check names, numbers and owners against the record

If the Fernhill summary said May 6, the team would plan the launch email around the wrong date. Read the draft against your notes, the recording or the transcript, line by line, and check:

- every name is spelled right and owns the right task
- every date and number matches what was said
- nothing marked as decided was still an open question

An AI-written summary needs the same check, because a note taker can attach a task to the wrong person or turn an open question into a decision. When an owner isn't clear from the record, ask them before you send.

### 7. Send it the same day

A summary sent the same day still matches what people remember. **Send it where your team already reads.** Use email for clients and people outside the team. For everyone else, post it in the team's Slack channel or the meeting's thread.

For an email, put the meeting name and date in the subject, attendees on To and anyone else on CC.

Keep it short enough to read on a phone, and save it with the meeting's other documents so it can be found later. For a weekly meeting, start the next summary with any action item still open from this one. Here's the Fernhill summary as an email:

```text
Subject: Website relaunch sync, May 2: summary

The relaunch moves to May 20 so the payments vendor can finish
testing the new checkout. Homepage photos are picked, and two
questions are still open: who writes the launch email and what
happens to old blog links.

Decisions
- Launch date is now May 20 (was May 6). The payments vendor
  needs two more weeks to test the new checkout.
- Homepage uses photo option B: [link to design]

Action items
- Confirm the May 20 date with the payments vendor (Marcus, Fri)
- Send the final homepage photo files to Marcus (Dana, Wed)

Open questions
- Who writes the launch email? Theo asks Lena by Friday.
- Do old blog links redirect to the new site? Marcus checks
  by next sync.

Next sync: Tuesday, May 9
```

In Slack, the same summary gets shorter. Lead with the overview in bold, and give each decision, action and question one line with the owner's @mention.

```text
*Relaunch moves to May 20* so the payments vendor can finish
testing the new checkout.
- Decided: photo option B for the homepage (link)
- @Marcus: confirm May 20 with the payments vendor, Fri
- @Dana: send final photo files to Marcus, Wed
- Open: launch email owner (@Theo asks Lena, Fri), old blog
  links (@Marcus, next sync)
```

## Common problems

### Nobody reads the summary

People stop at the first screen, so whatever sits below it goes unseen. Put the overview and decisions at the top, cut the summary until it fits on a phone, and link to longer documents instead of pasting them in.

### Someone says they never agreed to a task

The owner was probably guessed from the notes. Confirm owners before you send, or mark a task as "proposed owner" when you can't reach them, so they can turn it down in one line.

### The summary starts a thread of corrections

A correction thread means the draft wasn't checked against the record. Fix the summary once, resend it with one line saying what changed, and answer follow-up questions by updating that version.

### You didn't take notes

Write down every decision and task you remember right after the meeting, or [dictate them instead of typing](https://www.agent-native.com/use-cases/ai-dictation/). Then ask one attendee to check the list before you send. Next time, record the meeting, after telling the attendees, or keep a running list of decisions during it.

## Questions

### What should a meeting summary include?

A meeting summary has four parts: a two-sentence overview, what was decided, who does what by when, and what's still open. The reason for a decision and links to documents go in only when a reader would ask for them.

### How long should a meeting summary be?

A meeting summary should fit on one phone screen. The Fernhill example in this guide is about 100 words: a two-sentence overview and short lists of decisions, actions and questions. If it runs longer, the extra is usually discussion that belongs in the notes.

### What's the difference between a meeting summary and meeting minutes?

A meeting summary is a short, informal note of outcomes for the attendees and people who missed it. Minutes are the official record a board or committee keeps: attendance, motions and votes, approved by the group at its next meeting.

### When should you send a meeting summary?

Send a meeting summary the same day as the meeting, while people still remember it. Later, people act on their own memory of what was agreed, and the summary turns into a correction thread.

### Can AI write a meeting summary?

Yes. AI note takers, such as Agent-Native Clips, write a summary with action items from the meeting's transcript. Check it before you send it: confirm each owner and date against the transcript, and make sure nothing still open is listed as decided.
