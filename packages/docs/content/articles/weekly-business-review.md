To run a weekly business review (WBR), pick a short list of metrics, give each one an owner, and build charts that refresh on their own. Then meet for an hour each week and talk only about the charts that moved. This guide shows you how to set all of that up once, so the weekly work is reading the charts and deciding what to do.

## What you need

- Access to the tools that hold your numbers
- An owner for each part of the business
- A standing weekly slot

## How to run a weekly business review

### 1. Pick a short list of metrics

**Keep each part of the business to one screen**. Northpine, the made-up company in this guide, has two parts: customers, and money and product.

Each result the team is judged on is an output, like new monthly recurring revenue. Next to it, put an input the team can change before next week, like the share of trials that book a first meeting. Outputs show how you did; inputs show what to change.

Northpine's list starts like this:

| Metric | Input or output | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|
| New monthly recurring revenue | Output | Billing |
| Sales calls held | Input | CRM |
| Trial signups | Input | Product analytics |
| Trials that book a first meeting | Input | Product analytics |
| Customers lost | Output | Billing |
| Support tickets older than two days | Input | Support tool |

Leave out any metric nobody would act on if it doubled. If marketing sends a [weekly marketing report](https://www.agent-native.com/use-cases/weekly-marketing-report/), bring in only its headline numbers.

### 2. Give every metric an owner and a definition

Every number in the WBR needs one person who answers for it. **Write down how each metric is counted**: what's included, which tool it comes from, and which week it covers (for example Monday to Sunday, in one time zone). Add the owner and the definition as two more columns in the metric list.

The definition stops the meeting from arguing about the number when it should be talking about the business. When a definition changes, note the date, so a jump on the chart has an explanation.

### 3. Chart each metric against last year

A single number can't tell you whether this week was good. **Give every metric a weekly chart** of the last 13 weeks, with the same weeks last year and the target. Amazon's WBR made this format well known. Without a year of data yet, chart recent weeks against the target and add last year later.

For the few headline numbers, add a tile with this week's value and the change from the week before. Group the charts by part of the business, in discussion order, like the dashboard at the top of this guide.

Build it once, in a dashboard or a connected spreadsheet that refreshes on its own. Slides suit a monthly or quarterly review, but rebuilding a deck every week is the job that makes teams drop the WBR. Before the first meeting, walk the charts with the owners to agree the numbers.

### 4. Set the time, the people and the agenda

**Hold the WBR at the same time every week**, for an hour at most. Hold it early in the week, once last week's numbers are complete. Invite the metric owners and the people who decide what to do, and nobody who only listens. Pick one person to run it: they keep the order, keep time and write down the actions.

Write the agenda once, in the same order as the dashboard, and keep it in a shared doc that you reuse every week. For Northpine, it's:

1. Open actions from earlier weeks
2. Customers
3. Money and product
4. Anything the charts didn't show, if there's time

### 5. Have owners note what moved before the meeting

Each owner reads their charts by the end of the day before the WBR. **Write a short note for any chart that broke from its pattern**, under its section in the agenda doc. Say what you already know about the cause, or that you don't know yet and when you will. A chart that looks like its last few weeks needs no note, so the notes show where the meeting should go.

Northpine's signups owner, Dana, wrote: "Trial signups 412, up from 349. Most of the rise is organic search, starting the week the new pricing page went live. Checking landing pages by Wednesday."

Mark launches, price changes and outages on the charts as they happen, so anyone looking at a spike later sees its cause. If your numbers were wrong for a stretch, such as a broken tracking tag, mark that stretch too, so nobody acts on it.

![A weekly trial-signups chart with a key date on the x-axis for the pricing page launch on Oct 20, its hover card open. A grey band marks a week when signup tracking was broken.](https://cdn.builder.io/api/v1/image/assets%2FYJIGb4i01jvw0SRdL5Bt%2Ff4005f26b97f4bc881b056e66419e1b3)

### 6. Spend the meeting on what moved

Start with the open actions. Then walk the sections in the dashboard's order and **stop only where a chart has a note**. The owner explains it, and the room asks questions until the cause is clear or someone agrees to find it.

The charts are the agenda, so nobody presents. When someone starts walking through their team's projects, bring the room back to the chart and the number.

### 7. Give each flagged chart an owner

Each chart the room stopped on ends with **one person and a date to report back**. The person running the meeting writes these at the top of next week's agenda, such as "Landing pages behind the signup rise: Dana, Wednesday". If the room decides to wait and see, write that down too, with the week you'll look again.

### 8. Change the metric list on purpose

The metric list will change as you learn which inputs move your outputs. **Change it at a set interval**, such as your monthly business review, and never in the middle of a WBR. Drop metrics nobody has acted on for months, add the input you keep asking about, and update the owner and the definition when you do.

## Common problems

### A number jumped and nobody changed anything

The definition or the source changed: a new filter, a renamed event or a broken tracking tag. Check the chart's query or the tool it comes from. Fix the definition in your metric list, then mark the affected weeks on the chart so the meeting doesn't act on them.

### People talk about their projects instead of the numbers

The meeting has no rule for where to stop, so each person fills the time. Make the notes the rule: the room stops only on charts with a note. Project news goes in a written update sent before the meeting.

### Every metric is green and the meeting feels pointless

Either the targets are too low, or the list has only outputs, which move slowly week to week. Add the inputs behind each output, such as each step of your [signup funnel](https://www.agent-native.com/use-cases/ai-funnel-analysis/), and set targets that the team misses sometimes.

## Questions

### What is a WBR meeting?

A WBR, or weekly business review, is a recurring meeting about the same set of metrics every week. The people who run the business agree on actions for the metrics that moved. Amazon made the format well known, with its fixed metric order and owners who explain each change.

### What is the difference between a WBR and an MBR?

A WBR looks at last week's numbers to catch problems early and act on them. A [monthly business review (MBR)](https://www.agent-native.com/articles/monthly-business-review/) zooms out to trends, goals and the metric list itself. Teams that run both use the same metrics for each. For a customer-facing quarterly review, see [how to make a QBR presentation](https://www.agent-native.com/articles/qbr-presentation/).

### What metrics should a weekly business review include?

A WBR should include the few metrics that show how each part of the business did last week, and the inputs behind them. For a software company, that's usually signups, sales activity, revenue, customers lost and support backlog. Every metric needs an owner and a written definition.

### How long should a weekly business review be?

A weekly business review should take an hour at most, at the same time every week. An hour is enough when the work happens before the meeting: the charts refresh on their own, and owners have already written up what changed.

### Should a WBR be a deck or a dashboard?

A dashboard suits a weekly review better, because it refreshes from your data and nobody has to rebuild it. A deck suits monthly and quarterly reviews, which need a story around the numbers. If your team prefers slides, keep one fixed set of charts that refreshes, and avoid building new slides each week.
